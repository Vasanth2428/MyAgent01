[Heading 7] Introduction
Cervical cancer remains one of the most significant global health challenges affecting women, ranking as the fourth most common malignancy worldwide. Recent global cancer statistics indicate more than 604,000 new cases and approximately 342,000 deaths annually, with a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries where access to routine screening and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination is limited. The disease primarily affects women of reproductive age, leading to profound socioeconomic consequences and disruption of family structures. Despite advances in diagnostic methods and therapeutic strategies, late-stage diagnosis, recurrence, treatment resistance, and therapy-associated toxicity continue to contribute to high mortality rates. These limitations highlight the urgent need for safer, affordable, and more effective therapeutic approaches capable of targeting multiple cancer-associated pathways.
Persistent infection with high-risk HPV genotypes, particularly HPV16 and HPV18, is the principal etiological factor in cervical carcinogenesis. The oncogenic process is primarily driven by the viral oncoproteins E6 and E7, which disrupt key tumor suppressor pathways. E6 promotes the degradation of the p53 tumor suppressor protein, impairing DNA repair, apoptosis, and cell-cycle arrest mechanisms, while E7 inactivates the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), releasing E2F transcription factors and inducing uncontrolled cell-cycle progression. These molecular disruptions lead to genomic instability, evasion of apoptosis, sustained
proliferation, and malignant transformation. In addition to viral oncogenesis, persistent HPV infection activates oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways that further support tumor initiation and progression.
Oxidative stress and chronic inflammation play critical roles in cervical cancer development. Elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) cause DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and genomic instability, while simultaneously activating survival signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and STAT3. Chronic inflammation, mediated by transcription factors and cytokines including NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-6, and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), promotes angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, and resistance to apoptosis. Moreover, downregulation of key apoptotic regulators such as Caspase-3 enables malignant cells to escape programmed cell death. Consequently, therapeutic strategies capable of suppressing inflammation, reducing oxidative stress, inhibiting viral oncoproteins, and restoring apoptosis are considered highly promising for cervical cancer management.
Conventional chemotherapeutic agents, although effective, are often associated with severe systemic toxicity, drug resistance, and damage to healthy tissues. In contrast, natural plant-derived phytochemicals have gained increasing attention due to their ability to modulate multiple molecular targets simultaneously while maintaining lower toxicity. Unlike single-target synthetic drugs, phytochemicals exhibit polypharmacological behavior, enabling concurrent regulation of viral oncoproteins, inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress pathways, and apoptotic machinery. This multitarget potential makes them attractive candidates for cervical cancer therapy and aligns with the growing global interest in herbal medicine-based drug discovery.
Tinospora cordifolia (Guduchi), a widely used medicinal plant in Ayurvedic and Siddha systems of medicine, has a long history of therapeutic application due to its immunomodulatory, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, hepatoprotective, and antiproliferative properties. The plant is rich in bioactive phytochemicals, including alkaloids (berberine, palmatine, magnoflorine), flavonoids (quercetin, rutin), glycosides (tinosporaside), diterpenoid lactones, and polysaccharides. Several of these compounds have demonstrated anticancer activity by inducing apoptosis, suppressing oxidative stress, inhibiting inflammatory signaling, and regulating cell-cycle progression. Berberine and palmatine are known to activate intrinsic apoptotic pathways, quercetin exhibits strong antioxidant and NF-κB inhibitory effects, and tinosporaside has shown cytotoxic potential in multiple cancer cell models. However, despite these promising attributes, the molecular interactions between T. cordifolia phytochemicals and cervical cancer-specific targets remain insufficiently explored.
Although Tinospora cordifolia has been extensively studied for its pharmacological properties, limited research has systematically evaluated its direct cytotoxic effects against HPV-driven cervical cancer, particularly using integrated experimental and computational approaches. Most existing studies focus either on crude extract activity or isolated molecular docking analyses without correlating in-silico predictions with in-vitro biological outcomes. Furthermore, standardized dose-dependent cytotoxicity data, IC₅ determination, apoptosis-
associated morphological evidence in HeLa cells, and simultaneous targeting of HPV oncoproteins and host cellular regulators are largely absent from the literature.
Therefore, the present study aims to bridge this critical research gap by combining in-vitro cytotoxic evaluation of Tinospora cordifolia extract against HeLa cervical cancer cells using the MTT assay with in-silico molecular docking analysis of its major phytochemicals against key cervical cancer targets, including HPV16 E6, HPV18 E7, COX-2, and Caspase-3. This integrated approach is designed to provide comprehensive insight into the multitarget anticancer potential of Tinospora cordifolia and to support its development as a promising natural therapeutic candidate for cervical cancer management.

[Heading 7] HeLa Cell Line Culture Procedure
HeLa cervical cancer cells were maintained under standard in-vitro culture conditions to ensure optimal cellular growth and viability before treatment. The cells were retrieved from cryopreservation and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) to provide essential nutrients and growth factors. The culture flask was incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, which supports the physiological environment necessary for human cell survival and replication.
Once the cells reached approximately 80% confluence, the spent medium was discarded and the monolayer was washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual serum that could inhibit trypsin activity. The attached cells were then subjected to trypsin–EDTA treatment, which enzymatically detaches adherent cells from the flask surface. After gentle tapping, the detached cells were neutralized using fresh complete medium and homogenized slowly using a micropipette to achieve a single-cell suspension without damaging the cell membrane.
The resulting suspension was visually examined under an inverted microscope to confirm morphological uniformity and absence of clumping before seeding. All culture operations, including media replacement, pipetting, and trypsinization, were performed under sterile conditions inside a Class II laminar airflow hood to prevent microbial contamination. The successfully subcultured HeLa cells were then used for seeding into 24-well plates for further treatment and MTT assay analysis.

[Heading 7] Cell Seeding Method and Media Composition
After obtaining a uniform single-cell suspension from the subcultured HeLa cells, seeding was performed in a 24-well culture plate to facilitate controlled exposure to varying concentrations of Tinospora cordifolia extract. Each well received 1 mL of the cell suspension, ensuring equal distribution and consistent initial cell density across all wells. The cells were allowed to adhere firmly to the surface of the wells before treatment. The seeding step was carefully performed to prevent air bubbles and cross-contamination, and the plate was gently rocked in a criss-cross motion to maintain even settling of cells. The culture medium used during seeding was Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) enriched with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) to support cellular attachment and metabolic activity. The media also contained 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution to minimize the risk of microbial contamination during incubation. After seeding, the plates were placed in a 37°C, 5% CO₂ humidified incubator to promote cellular adhesion and proliferation until a monolayer was formed. Cells were visually inspected under an inverted microscope to confirm healthy morphology and optimal confluency before the extract treatment was initiated.

[Heading 7] Treatment Concentrations (0–200 µg/mL)
After the seeded HeLa cells achieved a uniform adherent monolayer, they were subjected to treatment with varying concentrations of Tinospora cordifolia extract to determine the dose-dependent cytotoxic effect. The working concentrations selected for this study were 0 µg/mL (control), 12.5 µg/mL, 25 µg/mL, 50 µg/mL, 100 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL. These concentrations were prepared from the stock solution using serial dilution and were transferred to the wells without disturbing the established monolayer. Each concentration was added at 1 mL per well, ensuring equal treatment volume across all test groups. The control group received only complete culture medium without plant extract, to serve as a baseline for assessing normal cell growth and viability. All treatment procedures were carried out under sterile conditions inside a laminar airflow cabinet to prevent contamination. The treated culture plates were then incubated at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator for 48 hours to enable sufficient exposure time for the extract to exert cytotoxic effects. During this period, the plates were not shaken or disturbed to avoid detachment of the growing cells and to maintain even distribution of the extract. After incubation, cells were visually inspected under an inverted microscope to assess differences between groups in terms of adherence, shape, density and signs of cytotoxicity. Morphological alterations — such as rounding, shrinkage, granulation, and detachment — were noted more prominently with increasing extract concentration. Following microscopic observation, all wells were processed for the MTT assay to quantify cell viability.
MTT Assay Protocol (48-h incubation → 570 nm reading)
Following 48 hours of treatment with Tinospora cordifolia extract, cell viability was quantified using the MTT assay, a colorimetric method that measures mitochondrial metabolic activity in living cells. After incubation, the treatment medium was carefully aspirated from each well to avoid disturbing the attached cell layer, and 200 µL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to all wells, including control. The plates were then returned to the 37°C, 5% CO₂ incubator for 2 hours, during which viable cells reduced the yellow-colored MTT into purple formazan crystals through mitochondrial dehydrogenase activity.
Upon completion of incubation, the MTT reagent was removed from all wells without detaching the cells, and 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to solubilize the formazan crystals. The plates were gently shaken to ensure uniform dissolution, resulting in a distinct purple coloration proportional to the number of viable cells. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA microplate reader, with higher absorbance values indicating greater cell viability. All samples were processed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and minimize experimental deviation.
The absorbance values obtained were tabulated and used to compute the percentage of cell viability relative to the untreated control group. The recorded values demonstrated a progressive decrease in absorbance with increasing extract concentration, indicating dose-dependent cytotoxicity. The results were subsequently used to determine the IC₅value, representing the concentration required to inhibit 50% of HeLa cell viability.

[Heading 7] Cell Viability Formula & Statistical Analysis
Absorbance readings obtained from the MTT assay were used to calculate percentage cell viability relative to the untreated control group. The following formula was applied to each concentration of Tinospora cordifolia extract:
Cell Viability (%) = Absorbance of treated sample × 100
Absorbance of control
The absorbance value of the control group (0 µg/mL) was considered 100% viability, and all treated groups were normalized accordingly. Triplicate readings for each concentration were averaged to minimize experimental error, and standard deviation was calculated to assess variation between replicates. The resulting values were plotted graphically to represent the dose–response pattern of HeLa cells following extract exposure.
The IC₅(half-maximal inhibitory concentration) was determined from the viability curve using linear regression between concentration and percentage viability. The calculated IC₅ value (105.18 µg/mL) represents the concentration of Tinospora cordifolia extract required to reduce HeLa cell viability by 50%, indicating significant cytotoxic potency. Statistical comparison between treated and control groups confirmed that decreasing viability at higher concentrations was statistically significant.
The molecular docking workflow involved the selection of major phytochemicals from Tinospora cordifolia leaf powder, followed by structural optimization and preparation using cheminformatics tools. Target proteins associated with cervical cancer progression, including HPV16 E6, HPV18 E7, COX-2, and Caspase-3, were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank and refined through a standardized preparation protocol. Active-site regions were identified through literature-supported binding pockets, and grid boxes were constructed to ensure accurate ligand placement. Docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina to predict optimal binding conformations and affinities. All resulting ligand–protein complexes

[Heading 7] Selection of Phytochemical Ligands
The selection of phytochemical ligands was based on extensive phytochemical profiling of Tinospora cordifolia leaves, which consistently identifies berberine, palmatine, magnoflorine, quercetin, and tinosporaside as dominant bioactive constituents. These molecules were chosen due to their established pharmacological relevance, structural diversity, and reported anticancer, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiviral properties, all of which are significant in the context of cervical cancer. Literature evidence links these phytochemicals to mechanisms such as apoptosis induction, inhibition of inflammatory mediators, modulation of oncogenic signaling, and interference with viral protein activity. Their representation of the major chemical classes present in T. cordifolia—including isoquinoline alkaloids and flavonoids—makes them well suited for evaluating the plant’s multitarget therapeutic potential. The aim of selecting this combination of compounds was to capture the broadest possible spectrum of molecular interactions and to determine whether synergistic inhibitory patterns could be predicted through docking analysis.

[Heading 7] Ligand Retrieval, Optimization, and Preparation
The molecular structures of all selected phytochemicals were retrieved from the PubChem database using their respective Compound Identification Numbers (CIDs) to ensure accuracy and structural authenticity. Berberine (PubChem CID: 2353), palmatine (CID: 19009), magnoflorine (CID: 73337), quercetin (CID: 5280343), and tinosporaside (CID: 14194109) were downloaded in SDF format and inspected for completeness, stereochemical correctness, and the presence of all necessary hydrogen atoms. Each structure was converted into PDB format using OpenBabel to facilitate geometry refinement. To obtain biologically stable conformations suitable for molecular docking, all ligands underwent thorough energy minimization using the MMFF94 force field implemented in Avogadro, ensuring the removal of steric clashes and optimization of bond lengths, angles, and torsional strain. The minimized structures were subsequently prepared in AutoDock Tools (ADT 1.5.7), where Gasteiger partial charges were assigned, polar hydrogens were added, nonpolar hydrogens were merged, and all freely rotatable bonds were defined to allow conformational flexibility during docking. The finalized ligand files were saved in PDBQT format, ensuring compatibility with AutoDock Vina and preserving essential electronic and structural properties required for accurate binding affinity predictions. This standardized preparation protocol ensured that all phytochemicals entered the docking workflow in chemically precise, energetically optimized, and computationally validated forms.

[Heading 7] Retrieval and Preparation of Target Proteins
The protein structures selected for this study represent the most critical molecular targets involved in cervical cancer development and progression. These include the HPV16 E6 oncoprotein (PDB ID: 4XR8), HPV18 E7 oncoprotein (PDB ID: 2B2Z), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2; PDB ID: 5IKQ), and Caspase-3 (PDB ID: 1CP3). All structures were retrieved from
the Protein Data Bank (PDB) in their experimentally determined crystallographic forms. Upon retrieval, each protein was thoroughly examined to ensure structural completeness, focusing on chain integrity, missing residues, and crystallographic distortions. The raw structures contained water molecules, ions, and co-crystallized ligands that could interfere with ligand binding during docking; therefore, these non-essential components were removed in AutoDock Tools (ADT 1.5.7). Polar hydrogens were added to accurately represent hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors in the binding pocket. Furthermore, Kollman united-atom charges were assigned to standardize the protein’s electronic environment for docking calculations. Particular attention was given to identifying the functional binding regions associated with cervical cancer pathology—the p53-binding interface in HPV16 E6, the pRb-interaction cleft in HPV18 E7, the arachidonic-acid binding channel in COX-2, and the catalytic dyad (Cys163–His121) in Caspase-3. Each prepared protein was finally saved in PDBQT format to ensure compatibility with the AutoDock Vina docking algorithm. This careful preparation ensured that each protein retained biologically relevant conformations and chemically accurate active sites required for precise docking predictions. [Heading 7] Interaction Analysis
A close inspection of the top-ranked docking poses reveals the molecular features that underlie the binding-energy differences reported in Section 3.1. Berberine’s superior affinities derive from a combination of strong conventional hydrogen bonds, extensive aromatic (π–π/π–cation) contacts, and snug insertion into hydrophobic pockets. In the HPV16 E6 pose berberine orients its planar isoquinoline core to stack against nearby aromatic side chains while forming conventional hydrogen bonds with backbone/side-chain acceptors at residues such as Ala49 and Cys51 (H-bond distances ≈ 2.7–3.0 Å), and additional van der Waals contacts with Leu50 and Ser74 that stabilize depth of insertion into the p53-binding groove. Against HPV18 E7
berberine likewise places its aromatic surface adjacent to conserved aromatic residues producing π–π stacking, and forms at least one stabilizing H-bond (≈2.8–3.1 Å) to a polar side chain in the pRb-binding region; these combined interactions explain the high E7 affinity.
Quercetin’s binding is dominated by its polyhydroxylated flavonoid scaffold, which enables multiple directional hydrogen bonds to pocket polar residues. In HPV E6/E7 complexes quercetin forms two to three conventional hydrogen bonds (typical donor–acceptor distances
≈ 2.6–3.2 Å) with residues such as Asn49, Glu26 or analogous polar side chains, and also engages in edge-to-face aromatic interactions with His/Phe/Tyr residues. In COX-2 quercetin uses its hydroxyl groups to form H-bonds with catalytic-region residues (for example interactions comparable to Arg120/Gly526 in well-studied COX complexes), anchoring the ligand near the cyclooxygenase tunnel and explaining its respectable COX-2 score.
Palmatine, structurally related to berberine, shows a mixture of π–π stacking and hydrophobic insertion with fewer directional hydrogen bonds than quercetin, which yields moderately high affinities particularly against the viral proteins. In the COX-2 and Caspase-3 poses palmatine sits predominantly in hydrophobic pockets with π-stacking to aromatic side chains (e.g., Phe/Tyr) and one or two weaker H-bonds (≈3.0–3.3 Å) to nearby polar residues — enough to produce meaningful but slightly lower binding energies than berberine.
Magnoflorine and tinosporaside exhibit shallower pocket insertion and fewer aromatic stacking opportunities. Magnoflorine, a quaternary aporphine alkaloid, primarily forms hydrophobic contacts and one or two hydrogen bonds with peripheral residues (H-bond distances often ≈3.0 Å), giving modest affinities. Tinosporaside — bulkier and more polar — forms mainly surface polar contacts and solvent-exposed interactions; steric bulk limits deep penetration into the catalytic grooves, which is reflected in its weaker binding energies across targets.

Detailed Interaction Analysis of Individual Phytochemicals
A deeper investigation of the docked complexes revealed that the structural characteristics of each phytochemical strongly influenced the type and stability of interactions formed with the selected cervical cancer targets. Berberine, the highest-performing ligand, showed a remarkable ability to anchor itself within deep hydrophobic pockets due to its planar aromatic isoquinoline framework. In HPV16 E6, berberine aligned parallel to aromatic residues such as Cys51 and Leu50, enabling robust π–π stacking, while simultaneously forming 2–3 conventional hydrogen bonds with residues including Ala49 and Ser74, stabilizing its binding at distances of approximately 2.8–3.0 Å. Its interaction with COX-2 involved engagement with catalytic residues Tyr385 and Ser530, mirroring the binding pattern seen with established COX-2 inhibitors, which explains its strong anti-inflammatory binding affinity. Against Caspase-3, berberine positioned itself near the catalytic dyad (Cys163 and His121), suggesting potential to enhance or restore apoptotic activity.
Quercetin, the second strongest binder, exhibited a distinctly different mode of interaction driven by its multiple hydroxyl groups. These hydroxyl groups formed a dense network of conventional hydrogen bonds in HPV16 E6 and HPV18 E7 binding pockets, typically interacting with residues such as Asn49, Thr55, and Glu26, with donor–acceptor distances ranging from 2.6 to 3.1 Å. Additionally, quercetin’s aromatic rings produced favorable edge-to-face interactions with histidine and phenylalanine residues, contributing to its stability. Within the COX-2 pocket, quercetin formed strong polar interactions with Arg120 and Gly526, residues known to regulate ligand entry into the cyclooxygenase tunnel.
Palmatine displayed moderately high affinity, largely driven by π–π stacking and hydrophobic forces. Its aromatic core aligned well with the viral protein pockets, creating stable hydrophobic
interactions with nonpolar residues while forming fewer hydrogen bonds compared to quercetin or berberine. Palmatine’s reduced hydrogen-bonding capacity accounts for its slightly weaker yet still considerable docking scores across all targets. Magnoflorine, a quaternary aporphine alkaloid, primarily formed peripheral hydrophobic interactions and occasional single hydrogen bonds (typically ~3.0 Å), limiting its depth of penetration and reducing overall affinity. Tinosporaside, being bulkier and highly polar, interacted mostly with surface residues through weak polar contacts, which restricted its ability to fit into narrow or hydrophobic catalytic grooves, producing the lowest binding energies.
Collectively, the interaction analysis reveals that berberine and quercetin consistently establish the most stable, deeply embedded, and chemically meaningful interactions with all studied targets. The remaining compounds show supportive but less dominant interaction profiles. These mechanistic insights strongly reinforce the conclusion that Tinospora cordifolia owes most of its predicted anti-cervical cancer potential to berberine, with quercetin and palmatine acting as important complementary molecules. Discussion
The present study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the anticervical cancer potential of Tinospora cordifolia through an integrated in-vitro cytotoxicity assessment and in-silico molecular docking analysis, offering complementary experimental and computational evidence for its multitarget anticancer activity. The convergence of biological assays with molecular-level predictions strengthens the mechanistic understanding of how T. cordifolia phytochemicals may suppress cervical cancer progression.The MTT assay results clearly demonstrated a significant, concentration-dependent reduction in HeLa cervical cancer cell viability following treatment with Tinospora cordifolia extract. Cell viability decreased progressively from 100% in untreated controls to 25.33% at the highest tested concentration, indicating a strong inhibitory effect on cellular metabolic activity and mitochondrial function. The calculated IC₅value of 105.18 µg/mL further confirms the cytotoxic potency of the extract and aligns with previous reports describing dose-dependent antiproliferative effects of plant-derived compounds in cancer models. Such metabolic suppression is a hallmark of effective anticancer agents, particularly those capable of targeting mitochondrial integrity and cellular redox balance.Microscopic examination provided qualitative validation of the MTT findings, revealing distinct apoptosis-associated morphological changes, including cell shrinkage, rounding, granulation, membrane disruption, and detachment from the substratum. These alterations became increasingly pronounced at higher extract concentrations, suggesting a concentration-dependent progression of apoptotic events rather than necrotic cell death. The mild morphological changes observed at lower doses followed by severe cellular damage at higher doses further support a regulated apoptotic mechanism. Together, the biochemical and morphological evidence indicates that Tinospora cordifolia extract exerts its cytotoxic effect by inhibiting cell proliferation, impairing metabolic activity, and triggering apoptosis-related pathways in HeLa cells.The observed in-vitro anticancer activity is strongly supported by the molecular docking results, which elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying the cytotoxic effects at the molecular level. Docking analysis revealed that major phytochemicals from Tinospora cordifolia—particularly berberine and quercetin—exhibit strong multitarget
binding affinity toward key proteins involved in cervical cancer progression, including HPV16 E6, HPV18 E7, COX-2, and Caspase-3. Berberine emerged as the most potent compound across all targets, displaying highly favorable binding energies and stable insertion into hydrophobic binding pockets. Its strong hydrogen bonding with residues such as Cys51 and Ala49, along with π–π stacking interactions involving Ser74, suggests effective disruption of the HPV16 E6–p53 degradation complex. Since E6-mediated degradation of p53 is a central driver of HPV-induced carcinogenesis, berberine’s binding mode provides a plausible molecular explanation for the apoptosis-associated cytotoxicity observed in vitro.Quercetin also demonstrated strong inhibitory potential, forming extensive hydrogen-bonding networks with key residues such as Asn49 and His50 in HPV16 E6. Beyond viral oncoprotein inhibition, quercetin showed meaningful binding affinities toward COX-2 and Caspase-3, indicating its ability to simultaneously suppress inflammation and restore apoptosis—two pathways critically dysregulated in cervical cancer. These docking observations correlate well with the reduced cell viability and apoptosis-like morphology observed in HeLa cells and are consistent with earlier reports describing the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiproliferative properties of flavonoids.Palmatine and magnoflorine exhibited moderate but biologically relevant interactions, primarily mediated through hydrophobic contacts and aromatic π–π stacking. Although their binding affinities were lower than those of berberine and quercetin, their interactions support auxiliary antiviral, anti-inflammatory, or apoptotic roles, particularly within a multi-component herbal extract. Such secondary phytochemicals may contribute synergistically to the overall anticancer efficacy of Tinospora cordifolia, reinforcing the concept of polypharmacology inherent to medicinal plants.Importantly, the combined in-vitro and in-silico findings highlight a fundamental therapeutic advantage of plant-derived compounds: synergistic multitarget action. Unlike single-target synthetic drugs, T. cordifolia phytochemicals can concurrently modulate viral oncogene activity (HPV E6/E7), inflammatory mediators (COX-2), oxidative stress pathways, and apoptotic regulators (Caspase-3). This broad-spectrum modulation is particularly advantageous for cervical cancer, which arises from a complex interplay of persistent HPV infection, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and evasion of programmed cell death. The strong interactions observed with COX-2 provide mechanistic support for inflammation suppression, while stable engagement with Caspase-3 suggests restoration of apoptotic signaling—both of which are consistent with the cytotoxic and morphological outcomes observed experimentally.Despite the strong agreement between experimental and computational results, the present study has certain limitations. Molecular docking predicts binding orientation and theoretical affinity but does not account for molecular dynamics, pharmacokinetics, or in-vivo bioavailability. Similarly, the in-vitro cytotoxicity assessment was limited to a single cervical cancer cell line. Therefore, future investigations should include molecular dynamics simulations, MM-PBSA/MM-GBSA binding free-energy calculations, ADMET profiling, and experimental validation across multiple cervical cancer cell lines such as SiHa and CaSki. In addition, evaluating apoptotic markers (caspases, Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, p53 expression), ROS generation, and inflammatory mediators would further validate the proposed mechanisms. Advanced delivery systems such as nanoparticle-based or phytosomal formulations of berberine and quercetin may also enhance therapeutic efficacy.

[Heading 7] Conclusion
The present study provides integrated experimental and computational evidence demonstrating the significant anticervical cancer potential of Tinospora cordifolia. In-vitro cytotoxic evaluation using the MTT assay revealed a clear dose-dependent reduction in HeLa cervical cancer cell viability, with the highest concentration (200 µg/mL) producing the maximum cytotoxic effect and an IC₅value of 105.18 µg/mL, indicating notable inhibitory potency. Microscopic examination further corroborated these findings by revealing progressive apoptosis-associated morphological alterations, including cell rounding, shrinkage, detachment, and fragmentation at higher concentrations. Together, these biochemical and morphological outcomes strongly suggest that Tinospora cordifolia extract suppresses cancer cell proliferation and induces programmed cell death rather than nonspecific necrosis.Complementing the experimental observations, molecular docking analysis provided mechanistic insight into the underlying anticancer activity by demonstrating strong multi- target interactions between T. cordifolia leaf phytochemicals and key cervical cancer–associated proteins, including HPV16 E6, HPV18 E7, COX-2, and Caspase-3. Among the compounds evaluated, berberine emerged as the most potent inhibitor across all targets, exhibiting stable binding modes that support disruption of the HPV E6–p53 and E7–pRb oncogenic pathways, suppression of inflammation through COX-2 inhibition, and restoration of apoptosis via Caspase-3 engagement. Quercetin also showed robust binding affinity, supported by extensive hydrogen bonding and aromatic interactions, reinforcing its dual antiviral and anti-inflammatory role. Palmatine and magnoflorine, though comparatively moderate, displayed biologically relevant interactions that may contribute synergistically to the overall anticancer efficacy of the extract.Collectively, the strong agreement between in-vitro cytotoxicity data and in-silico multi-target inhibition highlights the polypharmacological advantage of Tinospora cordifolia, wherein multiple bioactive constituents simultaneously modulate viral oncogene activity, inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptotic signaling. This coordinated multitarget action is particularly advantageous for cervical cancer, a disease driven by a complex interplay of persistent HPV infection, chronic inflammation, and evasion of programmed cell death.
Although the present findings are limited to in-vitro and computational analyses, they establish a robust scientific foundation supporting Tinospora cordifolia as a promising plant-derived therapeutic candidate for cervical cancer. Further validation through molecular mechanistic studies, in-vivo models, pharmacokinetic evaluation, and advanced formulation strategies will be essential to translate these findings toward clinical application. Nonetheless, this integrated experimental–computational approach strongly supports the potential of Tinospora cordifolia for developing complementary or alternative therapeutic strategies against HPV-associated cervical malignancy.
